Findings are organized by severity/type — structural risks, economic balance, timing, rules gaps, operational risk, and what's already working well — and tagged by confidence: Established stated directly in the source rules, Derived follows deductively from the rules, Risk a credible concern not yet quantified, Open a question the written rules can't settle on their own and needs simulation. Risk1. Room scheduling has no coordination mechanism

EstablishedEach round, 4 activity rooms × 2 guild slots = exactly 8 slots — one per guild. Over 4 rounds that's 32 slots, exactly enough for all 8 guilds to visit all 4 rooms once each.

DerivedNothing in the rules guarantees that happens on its own — guilds choose/race for rooms, constrained only by "two other guilds already paid their participation fee" locking a room. The system has zero spare capacity.

Derived — toy model(method in §7): under a policy where guilds pick, one at a time in random order, uniformly among rooms they haven't visited that still have a free slot — full knowledge of current availability, but no negotiation or anticipation — only 38% of games finish with every guild visiting every room. This is not a bound on the real event in either direction (real players might do better via negotiation, or worse from concurrent decisions and imperfect queue visibility) — it's evidence that an allocation problem exists under this policy, not an estimate of the event's actual odds.

Recommendation — a rotation that solves capacity and keeps opponents varied: found by constraint search and programmatically verified in simulation/rotation_schedule.py, this table keeps every guild visiting each room exactly once while guaranteeing no guild ever faces the same opponent twice, and no guild ever faces its own same-specialty twin:

Balance2. Economic balance

2.1 Starting hands: two feasible recipes, not one

EstablishedThe four Tier-1 materials form a 4-cycle: Flax–Wax → Candle, Wax–Charcoal → Dye, Charcoal–Saltpetre → Black Powder, Saltpetre–Flax → Cloth. Diagonal pairs don't convert to anything.

DerivedEach guild starts missing exactly one of the four types. That guild can only manufacture one Tier-2 card (it holds one unit of the shared "hub" item), but has a choice of two possible recipes:

Missing materialStarting materialsTier-2 choices (pick one)
FlaxWax, Charcoal, SaltpetreDye or Black Powder
WaxFlax, Charcoal, SaltpetreBlack Powder or Cloth
CharcoalFlax, Wax, SaltpetreCandle or Cloth
SaltpetreFlax, Wax, CharcoalCandle or Dye

This means simply dealing kits so exactly 2 guilds are missing each type gives a symmetrical opportunity set, not balanced demand — guilds still choose which of their two options to craft, and several could independently converge on the same one.

The deterministic fix: design starting hands against the room rotation (§1), guaranteeing each guild's hand supports its assigned first room's item:

This guarantees every guild can pay its Round-1 fee from its opening hand. It doesn't remove randomness from the player's point of view — the organizer picks which one material to withhold, but the specific 3 items a guild ends up with still aren't announced or predictable in advance.

2.2 Tier-2 items are admission rights, not 4-coin commodities

DerivedA Tier-2 item is also the entry ticket to a room that otherwise costs 15 coins cash. Its shadow value right before a required room visit is much closer to "coins saved" than to its 4-coin liquidation price. A guild holding two Tier-2 cards can craft a Tier-3 (8→14, +6) or hold one back to skip a 15-coin cash fee — depending on which rooms remain, the second can be worth far more. Crafting up the chain before room obligations are met can be a mistake, not a free win.

This game has time-dependent, endogenous item values, not a fixed price ladder — the simulation needs to model individual item identity, not aggregate counts (§2.5, §8).

2.3 The room reward loop makes the two admission paths very unequal

After completing a room, guilds exchange their entry item: "choose a new tier 2 item, different than the one they paid to participate."

  • Item admission: surrender a 4-value card → receive a different 4-value card. Net liquidation value unchanged (4→4), plus quest coins and possibly the 5-coin win bonus.
  • Coin admission: surrender 15 real coins → (apparently) receive the same reward card worth 4 in liquidation. Cash outflow is 15, but net wealth change is −11, not −15.

That's still a large asymmetry — holding the correct Tier-2 prerequisite may be one of the strongest strategic assets in the game. Worth being precise though: "net liquidation loss ≈ 0" is true in sale-price terms, but the strategic swing can be large either way — getting exactly the card you need next is a big win; being forced into one you don't (a reward-pool collision, §2.5) is a real loss even though the liquidation math nets to zero.

Two open questions: does a coin-paying guild also receive a reward card? And is the "3 available items" reward one pool shared by both guilds with cards removed as each picks, or does each guild see its own separate 3? Both added to §4.

2.4 Loans: the shortfall is usually much smaller than 15 coins

DerivedThe rule lends only the missing amount, not the full fee. Guilds start with 10 coins, so a guild needing the fallback with its starting coins intact needs a loan of only 5, repaid as 10 — not 30. A full 15-coin loan only happens after a guild has already spent down to zero.

OpenWhether a guild could be effectively out of contention within the first couple of rounds through loan debt alone is a real possibility but not something the written rules can establish — the severity depends on quantities the rules don't pin down, and needs to come from simulation. Loans aren't the only such mechanism — see §2.7.

2.5 The central open question: does this economy actually require trading?

OpenThe recipe cycle alone doesn't obviously force cross-guild trading.

DerivedA guild that never trades can craft one Tier-2 item from its starting hand, pay into Room 1, and receive a different Tier-2 as reward. If that reward matches an unvisited room, it can chain straight through all four rooms on its own inventory plus its own Tier-1 production — no other guild required.

What can break the chain: each room seats 2 guilds sharing one reward pool of 3 cards (an interpretation — see §4), winner picks first. If both guilds want the same next-room item, the loser is forced elsewhere — creating pressure to trade or fall back to a coin payment, though not strictly requiring it, since the guild could instead craft the item itself later.

Since networking is a stated purpose of the event, whether the rules create enough scarcity to force trading — or whether solo chaining can win outright — should be one of the first things the simulation measures, ahead of tuning loan interest or Tier-3 prices.

2.6 Guild special items and unique quests are marked "in elaboration"

EstablishedThe 20-coin guild-special bonus and each guild's unique quest aren't defined yet. Relevant to §2.4's catch-up question and guild identity — worth finishing before final simulation runs.

2.7 Loans aren't the only snowball mechanism

Two further mechanisms reward guilds for winning, not just penalize for losing — meaning they can compound with loans rather than offset them.

Derived1. The room winner picks their Tier-2 reward first. "The winners choose first from 3 available items, the losers choose second." A guild ahead on quest skill also gets first pick of its next strategic asset (§2.3) — determining whether it can chain to its next room (§2.5) or has to trade/pay coins instead.

Derived2. The +5 room-win bonus is positively correlated with quest skill, not independent of it. It's added on top of an existing lead, not distributed independently — a guild strong at a room's specific skills gets rewarded twice for the same underlying advantage.

OpenTogether with loans, that's three mechanisms that could all push the same direction: skilled/lucky guilds get more coins, better future inventory, and avoid debt, while weaker guilds get none of that and accrue interest too. Whether this compounds into a runaway lead, or is damped by other randomness, is exactly what the simulation should measure. The §1 rotation (no repeated opponents) at least stops any such compounding from concentrating repeatedly against one specific guild.

Worth testing directly: a rubber-banding variant where the loser picks their reward card first. Not a recommendation to adopt it — just a cheap comparison to isolate how much of the snowball comes from this one rule versus the +5 bonus or loans (added to §8).

Timing3. Timing

3.1 The schedule has zero global slack — the bigger risk

10 + 4×10 + 3×10 + 10 + 10 = 100 minutes exactly, with no contingency anywhere for a long briefing, a puzzle reset, a scoring dispute, AV trouble, or congestion. For a live 40-person event this outranks any single room's internal buffer. Recommend 5–10 minutes of recoverable slack somewhere in the program.

Gaps4. Rules ambiguities worth resolving first

The scoring contradiction is listed first since it affects simulation parameters directly, not just clarity.

  1. Quest scoring scale contradicts itself. The generic rule allows only 0/2/4/6/8/10, but the "1 coin per correct answer" quests (Music, Art, Languages) can score any value 0–10, and Cartographer's Workshop / Architect's Challenge scoring both include odd values (3, 5, 7). Only Locksmith's Secret is actually consistent with the generic rule.
  2. Does the 15-coin fallback also grant a reward Tier-2 item? (§2.3) — changes the relative cost of the two admission paths substantially.
  3. Is the "3 available items" reward one pool shared by both guilds (cards removed as each picks), or does each guild see its own separate 3? (§2.3, §2.5) — the shared-pool reading is what §2.5's scarcity argument assumes, but it isn't stated outright.
  4. Room assignment mechanism — free choice/racing, GM-directed, or (per §1) fixed rotation?
  5. Where/when can peer-to-peer trading happen? Crafting is Guildhall-only; general trades aren't location-restricted in the text.
  6. Common Quest 3B (Hall of Legends) doesn't state its clue count, unlike the other "identify 10 things" quests.
  7. Fallback for a guild that never completes all 4 rooms — a real risk at ~18% of guild-slots under one unmanaged-scheduling policy (§7) — that's a policy-specific baseline, not an event forecast.

Ops5. The Guildhall needs a designed, throughput-tested protocol

Conversions, fee payments, and loans all funnel through the Guildhall/GM — potentially handling production, two tiers of crafting, fee payment/booking, loans, and disputes for up to 8 guilds inside one 10-minute window, while guilds also negotiate trades in the same window.

"Add 2–3 helpers" is a reasonable start but isn't the real fix — the question is throughput, not headcount. Design an explicit transaction protocol (e.g. a self-service swap table for simple conversions, GM only for loans/disputes) and, if possible, time it in rehearsal: seconds per transaction, transactions needed per guild per round, and the probability a guild fails to finish its intended actions in 10 minutes.

Working well6. What's working well

  • The tier price ladder (§2.2) makes crafting a genuinely interesting decision once shadow value is accounted for — better design than "always craft," not worse.
  • Per-room quest timing comfortably fits the 10-minute slots when run in parallel (§3.2).
  • The room-level win/tie handling is simple to administer — 5 coins to the winner, 2/2 on a tie, no ambiguous edge cases in the arithmetic. (But see §2.7: simple to run isn't the same as balance-neutral — this bonus compounds with quest skill.)
  • Charity ticket design (fixed €20, no refund) is clean and doesn't interact with the in-game economy.
  • The two-guilds-per-room reward pool (§2.5) is a subtle, probably-intentional scarcity mechanism worth preserving — it may do more balancing work than the recipe graph itself.

7. Appendix: toy scheduling model (supporting §1, not the full simulation)

Method: 8 guilds, 4 rooms, 4 rounds, capacity 2/room/round. Each round, guilds process in random order and pick uniformly among rooms they haven't visited with a free slot — i.e. full knowledge of current availability, but no negotiation and no anticipation. 200,000 simulated games, seeded for reproducibility. Code: simulation/toy_scheduling_model.py.

MetricResult
Games where all 8 guilds visit all 4 rooms38.2%
Individual guild-slots that complete all 4 rooms82.3%
Guild-slots missing exactly 1 room17.6%
Guild-slots missing 2+ rooms~0%

Reading these numbers: not a prediction or bound on the real event — real players might do better (negotiation, visible queues) or worse (concurrent decisions, confusion, time pressure), and nothing here says which dominates. What it establishes: under one simple, fully-specified decentralized policy, exact capacity-equals-demand doesn't guarantee successful allocation. It's also scope-limited: this model only tests the room/seat mechanic in isolation, not its entanglement with which Tier-2 item a guild holds, coins, loans, or the previous room's reward (§2.1–§2.5) — deliberately, so 38.2% shouldn't be read as an estimate of the full economy. It exists to make the §1 risk checkable and to benchmark the fixed rotation, which reaches 100% by construction.

8. Simulation plan (methodology, before any code for the full model)

A simulation isn't useful design evidence unless its questions and comparison variants are specified before it's built.

Core questions to measure

  1. Probability every guild completes all 4 rooms under realistic (not worst-case) scheduling.
  2. Distribution of final scores — how much spread comes from starting-hand luck vs. room-assignment luck vs. play quality.
  3. Loans per guild and resulting debt distribution at game end.
  4. Inter-guild trades that actually occur, and the fraction of guilds that could finish competitively without trading at all (§2.5).
  5. Tier-1/2/3 inventory levels over time, per guild.
  6. Shadow value of each Tier-2 item type by round (§2.2), not its sale price.
  7. Marginal expected-score value of a single room visit, including its reward card and downstream effects — not just quest coins.
  8. Win-rate differences by guild/specialty, once §2.6's content is finalized.
  9. Sensitivity to player skill/behavior — rational optimizers vs. casual play.
  10. Compounding of the three snowball mechanisms (§2.7) — loans, winner's-first-pick, +5 bonus — measured together: how much of final-score spread traces to an early lead vs. genuinely independent luck later.
  11. Opponent and interaction diversity — distinct opposing guilds encountered, distinct trading partners, cross-specialty interactions. "Number of trades" alone doesn't measure networking — ten trades with one guild is a very different outcome from six spread across six guilds.
Before implementation: item 9 needs concrete agent behavior specs, not just a label. Without pinning down actual decision rules, a full simulator can produce precise-looking output that's really just an artifact of whatever behavior got implemented. Each agent policy needs explicit rules for: craft choice (when to convert vs. hold, §2.2), room-access valuation (holding an item for a room vs. liquidating it), trade initiation/acceptance (what price an agent offers or accepts), reasoning about other guilds' inventories, and reward-card selection (§2.3). "Rational" and "casual" should be two different concrete implementations of all five, not two labels on the same logic.

Design variants to compare

  • Fixed room rotation (§1) vs. free-choice scheduling — should make the opponent-repetition cost from §2.7 directly visible in the numbers, not just argued from principle.
  • Coordinated starting hands (§2.1) vs. purely random.
  • Current loan interest (double) vs. capped/first-loan-only.
  • Current Tier-3 sale values vs. alternatives.
  • Mandatory vs. optional trading windows (directly probing §2.5).
  • Winner-picks-first (current) vs. loser-picks-first vs. random order (§2.7's rubber-banding test).

Building the full model is the next step once §4's ambiguities are resolved — particularly the scoring contradiction and the coin-payment reward-card question, both of which change simulation parameters directly.

9. Priority order for fixes

  1. Use the room/opponent rotation in §1 — it satisfies capacity and opponent diversity together.
  2. Coordinate starting hands (§2.1) with the rotation from #1.
  3. Model Tier-2 items as admission rights (§2.2–§2.3), not commodities.
  4. Determine whether trading is actually necessary (§2.5) — directly relevant to the networking purpose.
  5. Model the three snowball mechanisms together (§2.7) before rebalancing any single one in isolation.
  6. Resolve the quest-scoring contradiction (§4.1).
  7. Clarify the loan rule, coin-payment reward, and shared-pool questions (§2.4, §4).
  8. Add global schedule slack (§3.1) — currently none.
  9. Design and throughput-test the Guildhall protocol (§5), not just add volunteers.
  10. Finalize guild special items and unique quests (§2.6).
  11. Build the full simulation against §8's plan, only once 1–10 are settled.
