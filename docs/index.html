European Legends — Office Adventure Game

An independent balance & design review of the ruleset — revised after external review — ahead of an economic simulation of the trading and crafting system.

Ruleset v.16 Aug 2026 8 guilds · 40 players 100-minute economy

0. What changed in this revision

An independent reviewer found three reasoning errors in the first draft, one of which invalidated a central recommendation, plus dynamics the first draft didn't examine at all. In order of how much they change the conclusions:

  1. §2.1 was mathematically wrong. A starting hand supports two feasible Tier-2 recipes, not one determined type. This breaks the original "deal kits to control room demand" recommendation — corrected below with a working replacement.
  2. §2.4's loan math overstated the penalty in the typical case, and "eliminated by round 2" was an unsupported claim — now an explicit open question.
  3. The whole economic framing was too simple — sale price ≠ strategic value. New §2.2–§2.3 on items as admission rights and the reward-exchange mechanic.
  4. A likely rules contradiction was missed in quest scoring — now top of §4.
  5. The scheduling "default outcome" claim was asserted, not measured — now backed by a toy Monte Carlo model (§7) plus a concrete, checked rotation table.
  6. A new central question now headlines the document: does this game actually require cross-guild trading? See §2.5.
  7. Timing (§3) now leads with zero global schedule slack, a bigger risk than any single room's buffer.
  8. §5 (Guildhall) reframed around throughput/protocol design, not headcount.

Risk1. Room scheduling has no coordination mechanism

Each round, 4 activity rooms × 2 guild slots = exactly 8 slots — one per guild. Over 4 rounds that's 32 slots, exactly enough for all 8 guilds to visit all 4 rooms once each. Nothing in the rules guarantees that happens on its own.

This is measured, not assumed (method in §7): a toy model of guilds picking randomly among available, unvisited rooms each round — no negotiation, no GM steering — produces the numbers above. The real event has a GM and visible queues, which should do better than this worst-case-ish model, but it shows the risk is real, not theoretical. An independent check by the reviewer using the same approach landed within 1 point of this figure (38.4%).

Recommendation — a concrete, checked rotation: splitting the 8 guilds into two groups of 4 and cycling each independently through the four rooms guarantees exactly 2 guilds/room/round and one visit per guild per room, by construction:

Balance2. Economic balance

2.1 Starting hands: two feasible recipes, not one — corrected

The four Tier-1 materials form a 4-cycle: Flax–Wax → Candle, Wax–Charcoal → Dye, Charcoal–Saltpetre → Black Powder, Saltpetre–Flax → Cloth. Diagonal pairs don't convert to anything.

Each guild starts missing exactly one of the four types. The first draft claimed the missing type determines a single craftable Tier-2 item. That's wrong — the guild can only manufacture one card (it holds one unit of the shared "hub" item), but has a choice of two possible recipes:

Missing materialStarting materialsTier-2 choices (pick one)
FlaxWax, Charcoal, SaltpetreDye or Black Powder
WaxFlax, Charcoal, SaltpetreBlack Powder or Cloth
CharcoalFlax, Wax, SaltpetreCandle or Cloth
SaltpetreFlax, Wax, CharcoalCandle or Dye

This invalidates the original recommendation to deal kits so exactly 2 guilds are missing each type — that controls the aggregate opportunity set, not what guilds actually choose. Four guilds could all pick Candle from four different hands. Controlled dealing gives symmetrical opportunity, not balanced demand.

The actual deterministic fix: design starting hands against the room rotation (§1), guaranteeing each guild's hand supports its assigned first room's item — always achievable, since each "missing" choice supports exactly two Tier-2 outputs:

This guarantees every guild can pay its Round-1 fee from its opening hand, without players noticing anything less random — they still don't know in advance which two items they'll get, only which one they won't.

2.2 Tier-2 items are admission rights, not 4-coin commodities

The first draft compared items purely by sale price (Tier 1 = 1, Tier 2 = 4, Tier 3 = 14) and concluded crafting up the chain is "strictly worth doing." That conflates sale price with strategic value — the biggest conceptual gap in the first draft.

A Tier-2 item is also the entry ticket to a room that otherwise costs 15 coins cash. Its shadow value right before a required room visit is much closer to "coins saved" than to its 4-coin liquidation price. A guild holding two Tier-2 cards can craft a Tier-3 (8→14, +6) or hold one back to skip a 15-coin cash fee — depending on which rooms remain, the second can be worth far more. Crafting up the chain before room obligations are met can be a mistake, not a free win.

This game has time-dependent, endogenous item values, not a fixed price ladder — the simulation needs to model individual item identity, not aggregate counts (§2.5, §8).

2.3 The room reward loop makes the two admission paths very unequal

After completing a room, guilds exchange their entry item: "choose a new tier 2 item, different than the one they paid to participate."

  • Item admission: surrender a 4-value card → receive a different 4-value card. Net asset loss ≈ 0, plus quest coins and possibly the 5-coin win bonus.
  • Coin admission: surrender 15 real coins → (apparently) receive the same reward card. Net loss ≈ 15 coins for the same reward.

That's a large asymmetry — holding the correct Tier-2 prerequisite may be one of the strongest strategic assets in the game. Open question: does a coin-paying guild also receive a reward card? The text reads as if it assumes item payment — added to §4.

2.4 Loans: the shortfall is usually much smaller than 15 coins — corrected

The first draft assumed every loan is 15 coins, doubled to 30. The rule lends only the missing amount. Guilds start with 10 coins, so a guild needing the fallback with its starting coins intact needs a loan of only 5, repaid as 10 — not 30. A full 15-coin loan only happens after a guild has already spent down to zero, which is possible later but isn't the round-1 default.

The stronger claim — "eliminated from contention by round 2" — was asserted without math. Honest version: loans can create negative feedback, but how severe it actually is depends on quantities the written rules don't pin down, and needs to come from simulation, not from reading the rules.

2.5 The central open question: does this economy actually require trading?

This cuts against the first draft's own claim that the recipe cycle "forces genuine cross-guild trading." A guild that never trades can craft one Tier-2 item from its starting hand, pay into Room 1, and receive a different Tier-2 as reward. If that reward matches an unvisited room, it can chain straight through all four rooms on its own inventory plus its own Tier-1 production — no other guild required.

What can break the chain: each room seats 2 guilds sharing one reward pool of 3 cards, winner picks first. If both guilds want the same next-room item, the loser is forced elsewhere — exactly where trading (or a coin fallback) becomes necessary. Scarcity, and the incentive to trade, may be concentrated entirely in these two-guild collisions rather than being constant pressure from the recipe graph.

Since networking is a stated purpose of the event, whether the rules create enough scarcity to force trading — or whether solo chaining can win outright — should be one of the first things the simulation measures, ahead of tuning loan interest or Tier-3 prices.

2.6 Guild special items and unique quests are marked "in elaboration"

The 20-coin guild-special bonus and each guild's unique quest aren't defined yet. Relevant to §2.4's catch-up question and guild identity — worth finishing before final simulation runs.

Timing3. Timing

3.1 The schedule has zero global slack — the bigger risk

10 + 4×10 + 3×10 + 10 + 10 = 100 minutes exactly, with no contingency anywhere for a long briefing, a puzzle reset, a scoring dispute, AV trouble, or congestion. For a live 40-person event this outranks any single room's internal buffer. Recommend 5–10 minutes of recoverable slack somewhere in the program.

Gaps4. Rules ambiguities worth resolving first

Reordered — the scoring contradiction is promoted to the top since it affects simulation parameters directly, not just clarity.

  1. Quest scoring scale contradicts itself. The generic rule allows only 0/2/4/6/8/10, but the "1 coin per correct answer" quests (Music, Art, Languages) can score any value 0–10, and Cartographer's Workshop / Architect's Challenge scoring both include odd values (3, 5, 7). Only Locksmith's Secret is actually consistent with the generic rule.
  2. Does the 15-coin fallback also grant a reward Tier-2 item? (§2.3) — changes the relative cost of the two admission paths substantially.
  3. Room assignment mechanism — free choice/racing, GM-directed, or (per §1) fixed rotation?
  4. Where/when can peer-to-peer trading happen? Crafting is Guildhall-only; general trades aren't location-restricted in the text.
  5. Common Quest 3B (Hall of Legends) doesn't state its clue count, unlike the other "identify 10 things" quests.
  6. Fallback for a guild that never completes all 4 rooms — now known to be a real risk at ~18% of guild-slots under unmanaged scheduling (§7).

Ops5. The Guildhall needs a designed, throughput-tested protocol

Conversions, fee payments, and loans all funnel through the Guildhall/GM — potentially handling production, two tiers of crafting, fee payment/booking, loans, and disputes for up to 8 guilds inside one 10-minute window, while guilds also negotiate trades in the same window.

"Add 2–3 helpers" is a reasonable start but isn't the real fix — the question is throughput, not headcount. Design an explicit transaction protocol (e.g. a self-service swap table for simple conversions, GM only for loans/disputes) and, if possible, time it in rehearsal: seconds per transaction, transactions needed per guild per round, and the probability a guild fails to finish its intended actions in 10 minutes.

Working well6. What's working well

  • The tier price ladder (§2.2) makes crafting a genuinely interesting decision once shadow value is accounted for — better design than "always craft," not worse.
  • Per-room quest timing comfortably fits the 10-minute slots when run in parallel (§3.2).
  • Room-level win bonus / tie handling (5 coins, or 2/2 on a tie) is simple with no weird edge cases.
  • Charity ticket design (fixed €20, no refund) is clean and doesn't interact with the in-game economy.
  • The two-guilds-per-room reward pool (§2.5) is a subtle, probably-intentional scarcity mechanism worth preserving — it may do more balancing work than the recipe graph itself.

Removed from this list: "the recipe graph forces cross-guild trading" — now an open question, see §2.5.

7. Appendix: toy scheduling model (supporting §1, not the full simulation)

Method: 8 guilds, 4 rooms, 4 rounds, capacity 2/room/round. Each round, guilds process in random order and pick uniformly among unvisited rooms with a free slot. No negotiation, no GM steering — the simplest possible model of fully unmanaged self-scheduling, used only to check the concern is measurable. 200,000 simulated games, seeded for reproducibility. Code: simulation/toy_scheduling_model.py.

8. Simulation plan (methodology, before any code for the full model)

A simulation isn't useful design evidence unless its questions and comparison variants are specified before it's built.

Core questions to measure

  1. Probability every guild completes all 4 rooms under realistic (not worst-case) scheduling.
  2. Distribution of final scores — how much spread comes from starting-hand luck vs. room-assignment luck vs. play quality.
  3. Loans per guild and resulting debt distribution at game end.
  4. Inter-guild trades that actually occur, and the fraction of guilds that could finish competitively without trading at all (§2.5).
  5. Tier-1/2/3 inventory levels over time, per guild.
  6. Shadow value of each Tier-2 item type by round (§2.2), not its sale price.
  7. Marginal expected-score value of a single room visit, including its reward card and downstream effects — not just quest coins.
  8. Win-rate differences by guild/specialty, once §2.6's content is finalized.
  9. Sensitivity to player skill/behavior — rational optimizers vs. casual play.

Design variants to compare

  • Fixed room rotation (§1) vs. free-choice scheduling.
  • Coordinated starting hands (§2.1) vs. purely random.
  • Current loan interest (double) vs. capped/first-loan-only.
  • Current Tier-3 sale values vs. alternatives.
  • Mandatory vs. optional trading windows (directly probing §2.5).

Building the full model is the next step once §4's ambiguities are resolved — particularly the scoring contradiction and the coin-payment reward-card question, both of which change simulation parameters directly.

9. Priority order for fixes

  1. Correct understanding of starting hands (§2.1) before designing any kit-dealing rule.
  2. Model Tier-2 items as admission rights (§2.2–§2.3), not commodities — shapes how the simulation values inventory.
  3. Determine whether trading is actually necessary (§2.5) — directly relevant to the networking purpose.
  4. Resolve the quest-scoring contradiction (§4.1) — needed before quest income can be modeled.
  5. Clarify the loan rule and coin-payment reward question (§2.4, §4.2).
  6. Implement the fixed room rotation, coordinated with starting hands (§1, §2.1) — table ready to use.
  7. Add global schedule slack (§3.1) — currently none.
  8. Design and throughput-test the Guildhall protocol (§5), not just add volunteers.
  9. Finalize guild special items and unique quests (§2.6).
  10. Build the full simulation against §8's plan, only once 1–9 are settled.
